## **Historical/Cultural/Geological Background**

Honouliuli isn’t your typical sunny Hawaiian postcard destination. Its tale is threaded with somber strands from World War II. Established as a national monument in 2015, it serves as a haunting reminder of one America’s darkest periods - the internment of Japanese Americans during WWII. 

The camp opened on March 2, 1943 and was dubbed "Jigoku-Dani," or Hell Valley, due to its sunless days and gloomy atmosphere brought by its location—a deep gulch hidden from view. It held about 400 civilian internees and 4,000 prisoners of war until its closure in 1946.

Today nature has largely reclaimed Honouliuli Gulch; overgrown plants mask concrete foundations and rusted barbed wire fences while birdsong fills the air instead of human voices. But still present is an aura of historic consequence that demands our attention.

## **Activities Guide**

Well, what does one do at a historic site that bears such profound stories? You pay tribute, you learn, and you pass on the tales. 

Guided tours are available to experience Honouliuli's history intimately. The expert storytellers walk visitors through remnants of internee living areas, mess halls, guard towers and more.

Once explored as a group, visitors are encouraged to wander independently. This solitary exploration further augments the reality of what occurred here.

Honouliuli's inclusion in the National Park System not only affirms its significance within Hawaiian history but also places it within a broader narrative of wartime internment camps scattered across US mainland states like California, Arizona and Colorado.

Visiting Honouliuli offers an individual narrative on Hawaii's role during the war years while simultaneously revealing how this single story adds up to America’s collective memory of WWII internment.

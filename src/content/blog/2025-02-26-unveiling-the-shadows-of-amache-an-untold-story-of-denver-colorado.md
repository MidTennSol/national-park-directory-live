## **Introduction**
Nestled amidst the sweeping landscapes near Denver, Colorado is a site that whispers stories of our nation's past - The Amache National Historic Site. It’s almost as if time has purposefully brushed its protective fingers over this place, preserving its memory in a quiet reverence. Once known as the Granada War Relocation Center during World War II where more than 7,000 Japanese Americans were unceremoniously interned, today it stands as a stark reminder of resilience and perseverance against adversity.

## **Historical/Cultural/Geological Background**
The name 'Amache' derives from the Cheyenne Indian word for "Little Meadow," reflecting its position within nature's grandeur. Yet contrastingly, between 1942 and 1945, Amache was anything but tranquil. In response to Pearl Harbor's attack, President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066 leading to Japanese-Americans’ mass incarceration across ten camps nationwide - with Amache being one such site.

The layout is startling even now - barracks-style housing blocks sprawled across some 10,000 acres divided by dust-blown roads trapped within barbed wire fences. Despite these bleak conditions, internees strived to uphold their traditions and community spirit; they formed schools and social groups while cultivating beautifully landscaped gardens around their homes.

Today, only silhouettes of this history remain - a cemetery, foundations of the barracks, the guard tower - each echoing tales of a time eclipsed by the shadows of war.

## **Activities Guide**
Despite its somber past, Amache offers several engaging activities. Begin your visit at the small museum in downtown Granada for an insightful prelude to your on-site visit. Here, artifacts such as internee-created art pieces and historical documents vividly narrate Amache's poignant tale.

Then head to the actual site where self-guided walking tours allow you to navigate around relics such as the water tower, barracks remains, and cemetery. Interpretive signs along these routes provide context to what once stood there. 

Every year in May, former internees and their descendants gather for an annual pilgrimage to honor those who lived and died here - a remarkable opportunity to hear firsthand accounts of life within these enclosures.

## **Introduction**
Welcome to Congaree National Park – a living testament to North America's old-growth bottomland hardwood forests. Here, sky-scraping trees stretch upward as if competing to reach the heavens. The park, preserving the largest tract of such forest in the United States, presents an eerie integration of swampy Southern landscapes and towering giants that would be more commonly found in the Pacific Northwest. But it's not just its unique blend of ecosystems that sets this South Carolina gem apart—it also holds an intriguing history and houses a plethora of recreational activities.

## **Historical/Cultural/Geological Background**
The Congaree National Park is steeped in human history dating back thousands of years to Native American tribes like the Congaree people who once relied on these fertile lands for their livelihood. Later, escaped slaves used this area as a refuge—a magical place where freedom touched them before any emancipation proclamation did.

Geologically speaking, Congaree is part river floodplain part ancient hardwood forest producing towering loblolly pines and bald cypress whose girths challenge four adults arms linked together for a hug! Nestled within one immense meander of the Congaree River, this national treasure is one giant floodwater sponge which breathes life into every nook and cranny twice each year when it floods.

## **Activities Guide**

For the nocturnal adventurers, delight in the park's Night Sky programs that reveal an untainted view of the cosmos away from urban light pollution. ## **Visitor Information**
The park, located just outside Columbia, South Carolina, is open year-round and requires no entrance fee—making it easily accessible for families on a budget. Visitors Center hours vary throughout the year so do check their official website for accurate information. Facilities include picnic areas, campsites (with proper permits), restrooms, and water fill stations.

## **Tips for Different Visitors**
If you're planning a trip to Congaree National Park:

Family visitors: Pack a picnic lunch to enjoy at the Picnic Shelter after your morning hike.

Nature enthusiasts: Late winter and spring offer prime bird-watching opportunities as migratory species flock here.

Photographers: The Boardwalk Loop provides stunning vistas capturing reflections off still waters underneath towering trees—an unmissable photographic opportunity!

Hikers/Campers: Plan ahead as camping requires permits. Hiking in summer can be challenging due to humidity and mosquitoes–so come prepared with repellents and ample water.
